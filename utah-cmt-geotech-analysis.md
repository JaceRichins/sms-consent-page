### 2.2 How public on-call work is actually awarded [VERIFIED throughout]

- UDOT runs **exactly four** prequalified consultant pools; there is **no standalone CMT pool** — materials/geotech consultants enter through work disciplines inside the **General Engineering Services Pool**, which both UDOT **and Utah local governments** select from (one gateway, two client bases).
- Current GE pool cycle: **2025–2030** (Structural & Geotech pool: 2024–2028). A mid-2026 entrant just missed the advertisement. **Whether UDOT allows mid-cycle pool additions is an open, decisive diligence question** — call UDOT Consultant Services week 1.
- Awards from the pool go through only two methods: Small Purchase Contracts or **RPLOQ**, where the UDOT/local-government PM shortlists and invites only **3–5 firms** in the discipline (band roughly >$120K to <$450K per current-cycle documents). Selection is **Qualifications-Based Selection — price cannot win it**; PM relationships and discipline standing drive deal flow. Historic per-contract caps are small ($150K small-purchase cap in the 2016–2019 cycle). UDOT also imposes a cumulative per-consultant cap, deliberately spreading work.
- **Implication:** UDOT/municipal on-call is a credibility-and-volume channel with capped upside, structurally favoring incumbents in-cycle. It should be the *second* act. The *first* act is private/commercial special inspections + CMT, where the buyer (owner/architect/SER) hires per-project.
- Municipal cadence example: Salt Lake City ran an on-call material-testing procurement (**Job No. ONC25055**, SOQs due Oct 8, 2025). Award and rate schedule are pullable public records — 90-day item (GRAMA request if not posted).
- Salt Lake City special-inspection process [SOURCED — slc.gov]: inspectors must be **independent of the contractor** whose work they inspect; a signed Special Inspector Agreement is uploaded through the city's PDOX system; a pre-construction consultation inspection ("197") with the special inspector, foreman, contractor, and city inspector is required. This city-administered gate is exactly where a new firm becomes visible to building officials.

## 3. Workstream 2 — Entry routes

### 3.1 Greenfield: cost ~$250–500K, **12–18 months to accreditation-gated revenue** [mechanics VERIFIED]

The accreditation critical path is now precisely characterized:

1. Register with AASHTO re:source; implement an **R18 quality-management system**; enroll in required **proficiency sample programs** (rounds ship on annual-ish cycles — a missed round costs months).
2. On-site assessment is scheduled on AASHTO re:source/CCRL **regional tour cycles, not on demand** — calendar risk is theirs, not yours.
3. After assessment: final report in **~4 weeks**; all nonconformities must be resolved **within 60 days**; accreditation follows.
4. Nuclear density gauges: **Utah is an NRC Agreement State (since April 1, 1984)** — the license comes from **Utah DEQ, Division of Waste Management and Radiation Control**, not the NRC. [VERIFIED] Timeline a few weeks–3 months; fee ~$440 (R313-70); RSO training is a one-day class but some Agreement States require it before issuance — sequence it first. Reciprocity allows ~180 days/yr of out-of-state work (WY/ID/NV day-trips fine; sustained multi-state needs per-state licenses). Troxler's license-exempt EGauge is a possible shortcut **only if** UDOT/project specs accept it — unverified, treat as upside.
5. People: ACI field/lab certs (exam-based, fast) and ICC special-inspector certs per discipline; Utah adds a **DOPL building-inspector licensure layer** that incumbents' SI staff carry [SOURCED — verify scope of when it's required, week 2]. ## 4. Workstream 3 — Ownership law and the PE problem

### 4.1 The load-bearing legal question: **RESOLVED GREEN** [VERIFIED to high confidence; get an opinion letter anyway]

- **Utah Code Title 58, Chapter 22** (Professional Engineers and Professional Land Surveyors Licensing Act) licenses **individuals only**. DOPL's engineering program has **no firm-license or certificate-of-authorization category at all** — the laws-and-rules page lists only 58-1, R156-1, R156-46b, 58-22, R156-22.
- **58-22-305(1)(g)** exempts an organization practicing engineering from licensure provided all individuals who practice are licensed or exempt; **58-22-602** (organization/forms-of-practice provision) lets any business entity practice through licensed employees in responsible charge; **58-22-601/603** put sealing duties on the individual licensee. Nothing in the chapter restricts who may own the entity.
- The only Utah licensee-ownership rule lives in the **Professional Corporation Act (Title 16, Ch. 11)** — 16-11-8 limits PC shareholders to same-profession licensees — and it binds **only if you elect the PC form**. 16-11-15 expressly permits organizing under the ordinary corporation statutes instead. **Structure: standard Utah LLC (or C-corp), non-engineer majority owner, PE(s) in responsible charge.** Contrast states requiring firm COAs or PE ownership (FL, TX, NC, ID, NJ) — Utah is among the friendliest states in the country for this structure, which is itself a mild tailwind for headquartering the platform here.
- Constraints that DO bind: R156-22 responsible-charge/supervision standards govern what the unlicensed owner cannot direct (engineering judgments); the PE seals geotech reports; and marketing "engineering services" without a licensee would be unlawful practice. **CMT/SI-only operation without any PE is feasible** for inspection/testing scope (reports typically reviewed/accepted by the building official; some jurisdictions and most geotech deliverables want a stamp) — but it caps you out of geotech design, UDOT geotech disciplines, and report-stamping, so plan on the PE from the start.
- Residual verification (week 1–2): read the official 58-22 chapter text at `le.utah.gov` (proxy-blocked here; corroborated via multiple independent extracts), and obtain a Utah-licensed attorney's opinion letter covering 58-22-305/-602, R156-22, and Title 16-11 avoidance. Budget: $3–7K.

### 4.2 Recruiting the PE: cheaper than feared

Utah is the **lowest-paying state in the country for geotechnical engineers** [SOURCED — ZipRecruiter/Salary.com, July 2026]: average $84.5K; senior ~$110.5K; Geotech Engineer IV ~$122K; "principal engineer" averages $134K (ZipRecruiter) with Glassdoor's principal figure at ~$225K reflecting big-firm/tech-adjacent titles.

**Offer architecture [ESTIMATE]:** $140–180K base + 10–20% bonus for a senior Utah-licensed geotech PE as named principal/responsible charge — a visible premium over market — plus **5–15% real equity** vesting over 4–5 years, with key-person insurance and a second PE hired by month ~18 to kill the single-stamp risk. All-in year-one cost ≈ $200–250K. This is the single most important hire and the market test in the 90-day plan (10 sourced conversations) is designed to price it precisely. Note PE licenses renew March 31 of odd years — verify standing at offer.

## 6. Workstream 5 — Where software/AI genuinely compounds

The vendor landscape is mature: **MetaField (Agile Frameworks)** is the de-facto CMT LIMS + field-workflow platform; **ForneyVault** does machine-integrated concrete testing (auto-capture from break machines) with ForneyField; ElmTree, eFieldData, and AASHTOWare round out the market. [SOURCED]

**Implication — the honest one:** a LIMS is a **commodity feature, not a moat**. Any incumbent can buy MetaField (~$100–300/user/mo class pricing). Certerra explicitly markets itself as "technology-enabled." Software is not your product and not your differentiation *as software*.

Where it still compounds, because incumbents underuse what they buy:

1. **Dispatch density** — CMT margin is a routing problem (2-hr minimums + drive time). A scheduling engine that lifts field utilization from ~58% to ~66% adds roughly 4–6 points of EBITDA. Durable only while you're the best operator of it.
2. **Report turnaround as the sales weapon** — same-day AI-drafted, PE-reviewed field/SI reports (photos, gauge readings, spec citations auto-assembled). Buyers (SERs, owners' reps) switch agencies over turnaround and defensibility, not price. "Reliable and defensible" is literally how incumbents position [SOURCED — UTE].
3. **Back-office compression** — auto-invoicing tied to field events, cert/PSP/calibration compliance tracking, collections. Worth 2–4 margin points at 20+ staff.
4. **The data asset** — a decade of geotagged soils/compaction/break data across the Wasatch Front has quiet value (bid intelligence, risk pricing, eventually acquirer diligence candy).
5. **M&A integration playbook** — if you become the tuck-in buyer later, a clean operating stack is what makes 4–6x acquisitions accrete.

Expected effect at 20 staff: **+5–8 EBITDA points and faster share gain** vs. equally-sized incumbents — meaningful, compounding, but an operator's edge rather than a defensible technology position. Do not build a LIMS to sell; run MetaField-or-similar early, then replace components where your automation is genuinely better.
